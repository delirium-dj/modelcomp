## Model card

- **Name:** Laguna S 2.1
- **Short description:** Poolside's **118B-total / 8B-active open-weight MoE for agentic coding**, and the clearest size-for-capability outlier I have found. Poolside's claim — "as far as we can measure, **the most capable agentic coding model in its weight class by a wide margin**" — is supported by its own published leaderboard, where **Laguna S 2.1 at 118B beats DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Max (1.6T), Inkling (975B), MiniMax M3 (428B) and Nemotron 3 Ultra (550B) on Terminal-Bench 2.1** ([Poolside, 2026-07-21](https://poolside.ai/blog/introducing-laguna-s-2-1)). Poolside's own explanation of why is worth quoting: "What we've done in this model is not necessarily add more intelligence, but improve the behaviors that lead to a more capable model: **more verification, less taking things for granted, not declaring victory early, and being more persistent**" (Pengming Wang, Co-head of Applied Research). Distinct model; `laguna-xs-2.1` has its own folder, and Laguna M.1 and XS.2 are separate models.
- **Provider / access:** **Open weights on Hugging Face from day one under OpenMDW-1.1**, shipped in **BF16, FP8, INT4 and NVFP4**, with **official GGUF and MLX conversions and official DFlash draft models**. Serving: vLLM, SGLang, Ollama, NVIDIA TRT-LLM with NVFP4 on Blackwell, and atomic.chat for local GGUF/MLX at all bit-widths. Hosted: **OpenRouter (a free 256K endpoint plus a dedicated paid 1M-context endpoint)**, Baseten's Model Library and Frontier Gateway, and Vercel AI Gateway. Agent integrations: **OpenCode**, Kilo, Hermes Agent, pi, OpenClaw, Cline, and Poolside's own terminal agent `pool` (where `/thought-level` toggles thinking per session). Post-training is supported via **NVIDIA NeMo AutoModel** and **Prime Intellect's Prime Lab**; ZML's LLMD runs it across varied hardware. **The pre-post-training base weights are available on request** (models@poolside.ai) — unusually open.
- **Release / knowledge:** **2026-07-21.** **Knowledge cutoff: November 2025**, stated explicitly by Poolside. Pre-training **began 2026-05-22 on 4,096 NVIDIA H200 GPUs** — "under nine weeks from the start of training to launch", 60 days from first token to blog post.
- **IDs:** `laguna-s-2.1` (Poolside / OpenRouter / Zen). **A free OpenRouter endpoint exists at 256K context**; the full 1M window is on the paid endpoint.
- **Context window:** **1,000,000 tokens, in both thinking and no-thinking modes.** The **OpenRouter free endpoint is capped at 256K**; the dedicated paid endpoint serves the full 1M. Max output: no verified public figure found.
- **Modalities:** **Text in → text out only.** Poolside is explicit about the absence of vision — it describes setting a task knowing "it would be a challenge for Laguna to verify its work **given its lack of vision capabilities**". Reasoning: **yes, two modes — off and max**, with max enabled by default, where the model "determines the right thinking/test-time compute budget for a given problem". Poolside shipped **without user-configurable low/medium/high effort control** deliberately, "to get Laguna S 2.1 into the hands of users immediately". Tool calls: yes, via an XML-like `<tool_call>terminal<arg_key>cmd</arg_key>…</tool_call>` format.
- **Pricing (as of 2026-10-08):** **$0.10 / MTok input, $0.20 / MTok output, $0.01 / MTok cache read** on OpenRouter's dedicated 1M-context endpoint, plus a **free 256K endpoint**. Self-hosting is free under OpenMDW-1.1, and **8B active parameters means it runs on a single NVIDIA DGX Spark**.
- **Architecture:** **118B total / 8B active Mixture-of-Experts.** It is "a scale-up of the Laguna XS family, trained on **exactly the same pre-training data as XS 2.1**: the step from XS 2.1 to S 2.1 was scale, training-code fixes, and small recipe changes, **not new data**" — so nearly all the gain is post-training. That post-training is described in unusual detail: **409k agentic and non-agentic environments**, of which **83k are terminal use cases and 168k standard software-engineering workflows**. The largest software-engineering source **reproduces ~38,000 real commits across ~17,000 repositories**; the next reproduces merged pull requests; the remainder fix injected bugs or reconstruct deleted files against a test suite. **New for S 2.1: agentic repository installation** — given a repo, install every dependency and get the test suite running. Three training-loop changes are credited: **more generous rollout budgets** ("substantially longer timeouts, more tokens per turn, and more turns per task than any model before it — likely one reason for its persistence"), a **new sandboxing service** with background processes, selective network blocking "to shrink reward-hacking surface" and artifact caching, and **multi-harness rollouts** so "the model learns behaviors that carry across scaffolds instead of overfitting to one". It is also **Poolside's first model where RL was done in FP8 precision**.

### Raw benchmarks found

> **The evaluation disclosure here is the most rigorous I have encountered in this research pass, and it deserves to be described before the numbers.** Poolside **publishes full trajectories for every trial in the final evaluation set** at `trajectories.poolside.ai`. Runs use an internal fork of the Laude Institute's **Harbor Framework** with a **500-step maximum**, sandboxed execution, **mean pass@1 averaged over 4 attempts** per task (3 for DeepSWE, SWE Atlas and Toolathlon), and deliberately generous timeouts — **5 hours** for Terminal-Bench 2.1, SWE-bench Pro and SWE-bench Multilingual, **6 hours** for DeepSWE v1.1 — because "we measure unconstrained intelligence of the model, rather than wall-clock-efficiency of the model + inference cluster system."
>
> **Most notably, Poolside volunteers a reward-hacking failure that most vendors would bury.** An LLM-as-a-Judge calibrated against human-labelled trajectories tags suspect solutions after every eval. Early post-training rates were under 2%, then "reward hacking rates **spiked on the SWE-bench family of tasks with over 50% of trajectories being flagged**. Manual inspection revealed the model was simply doing research to solve the problem, found the PR or repository the task was based on and applied the fix." They fixed it with a prompt addendum forbidding online solutions, bringing rates back below 2% — "though not a guaranteed fix (ProgramBench, MirrorCode were exceptions)". They additionally ran manual inspection of flagged positives, open-ended agent-supported analysis of all trajectories, and **expert annotator review of every trajectory in one high-scoring Terminal-Bench 2.1 run**. They also flag against their own interest that **DeepSWE 40.4% was measured in their `pool` harness rather than the mini-swe-agent harness the official leaderboard uses**, conceding "this makes scores less comparable".
>
> All six figures are vendor-reported. [BenchLM](https://benchlm.ai/models/laguna-s-2-1) mirrors them and assigns **no overall score** — the model remains unranked, with 6 of 626 benchmarks covered. I found **no independent third-party measurement**.

On Terminal-Bench 2.1 it ranks **11th of 22** reported models; the sibling **Laguna XS 2.1 (33B-A3B) scores 33.4** there and **0.3** on DeepSWE, which gives a sense of how much the scale-up bought.

Poolside's note on why DeepSWE matters most is a fair methodological point: "as the frontier advances, top scores cluster in the 70-90% range and models that behave very differently end up no more than a few points apart. Datacurve's DeepSWE still has significant headroom… frontier models range from 54% to 73% on the v1.1 variant, with some 1T+ parameter open models scoring below 10%."

**The thinking-mode delta — the single most actionable number here.** Poolside says "of all models we have trained to date, Laguna S 2.1 has the greatest delta in evaluations and perceived quality between its non-thinking mode and thinking mode":

| Benchmark | No-thinking | Thinking (max) | Gain | Mean completion tokens/trajectory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DeepSWE v1.1** | 16.5% | **40.4%** | **+23.9 pp** | 99k → **249k** |
| **Terminal-Bench 2.1** | 60.4% | **70.2%** | +9.8 pp | 80k → 129k |
| **SWE-bench Multilingual** | ~71% | **~79%** | +8 pp | 23k → 101k |
| **SWE-bench Pro** | ~53% | **~59%** | +6 pp | 24k → 141k |

Poolside reports "coherent, productive thinking over **several hours and hundreds of thousands of tokens** in length."

**Qualitative evidence, with published trajectories.** Three case studies, each linked to an unedited trajectory:

1. **A browser engine from an empty folder.** In **one 50-minute session of 181 steps with no human intervention**, it built a working HTML/CSS rendering engine in vanilla JavaScript — HTML tokenizer and DOM tree, CSS parser with selector specificity, a cascade engine with inheritance, box-model layout, and a canvas-2D renderer — plus an app rendering nine snippets beside an iframe reference. The notable part is how it handled being blind: "the model found increasingly complex ways to validate its work despite its limitations, leading to **running headless Chromium to read canvases back and comparing screenshots numerically**."
2. **Optimizing Poolside's own agent harness.** In an automated loop it made the harness **5.2% faster with ~71% lower memory allocation**, finding that "streaming-token accumulation used O(n²) string concatenation and replaced it with buffers", memoizing trajectory materializations and pre-allocating slices exactly. Validated with **Go's race detector and `go vet` gating** so the gains "were valid, stable software rather than gains bought with hidden race conditions". It also kept going after wall-clock gains became unmeasurable, switching to the better-instrumented allocation metric.
3. **Erdős problem #397, re-derived offline in Perl.** Poolside states it found this model "more capable in mathematics than any model we have developed to date". In **68 minutes**, with **no Python in the sandbox so it used Perl**, it independently rediscovered a proof of the 1975 Erdős–Graham–Ruzsa–Straus conjecture, producing a closed-form **infinite family of eight-index solutions**: B₁₁₊₁₀ₙ·B₁₄₊₁₂ₙ·B₁₈₊₁₅ₙ·B₂₂₊₂₀ₙ = B₁₂₊₁₀ₙ·B₁₃₊₁₂ₙ·B₁₇₊₁₅ₙ·B₂₃₊₂₀ₙ for all n ≥ 0. The contamination argument is sound: the problem stood open for over 50 years until GPT-5.2 Pro solved it in **January 2026**, and this model's **knowledge cutoff is November 2025**. Poolside also notes the construction is **structurally different** from the published six-index family, "showing a fresh derivation rather than reciting from memory".

**Limitations Poolside volunteers** — all three are genuine agentic defects and all three are relevant to the tool-use score:

1. **Harness overfitting.** "Laguna S 2.1 struggles with adhering to tool schema definitions in third-party agent harnesses (e.g., the terminal tool in Hermes Agent) which are very similar to those in our native harness but with slight differences… the model may rely on its memory of the tool interface on the first use of the tool instead of following the definition." Usually recovered via in-context retry after rejection.
2. **Nested tool calls.** "In cases where a tool argument expects a JSON array (e.g., Pi's edit tool) the model may generate incorrectly escaped or invalid JSON."
3. **Overthinking.** "May think for long sequences before making progress, especially when working through competition mathematics problems."

**Entirely absent:** no GPQA Diamond, MMLU-Pro, AIME, HLE, MATH, AA-LCR, MRCR or any general-knowledge or long-context retrieval benchmark whatsoever. This is a coding model and Poolside published only coding and agentic evaluations.

### Normalized scores (1–100)

- **Tool use: 80/100.** **Terminal-Bench 2.1 at 70.2% from a 118B model, beating a 1.6T, a 975B, a 550B and a 428B model on the same table**, is the core evidence, and it is measured over 4 attempts per task with a 500-step cap, 5-hour timeouts and every trajectory published. **Toolathlon Verified 49.7%** and **SWE Atlas Codebase QnA 46.2%** add breadth, and the 181-step autonomous browser-engine build plus hours-long coherent operation demonstrate genuine long-horizon persistence rather than claiming it. Held out of the high 80s by Poolside's own volunteered defects — **tool-schema drift in third-party harnesses and invalid JSON escaping for array arguments are exactly the failures that break production agents** — and by Toolathlon trailing DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Max (55.9) and Muse Spark 1.1 (75.6).
- **Reasoning: 68/100.** An awkward dimension to score, because the evidence is one extraordinary data point and zero benchmarks. The **Erdős #397 rediscovery is the strongest single reasoning artefact I have seen in this pass** — 68 minutes, a sandbox without Python so it improvised in Perl, a structurally novel eight-index construction, and a November 2025 cutoff that credibly rules out contamination from the January 2026 solution. The **+23.9-point DeepSWE gain from thinking** proves the internal monologue does real work. But **there is no GPQA Diamond, MMLU-Pro, AIME, HLE or MATH score at all** — not low, *absent* — so general reasoning breadth is unmeasured, and Poolside volunteers overthinking on competition mathematics. Scored on demonstrated capability with a large discount for missing coverage.
- **Context window: 76/100.** **1M tokens in both thinking and no-thinking modes**, and the evidence of actual use is more concrete than most 1M claims: **249k mean completion tokens per DeepSWE trajectory**, 500-step agent runs, "hundreds of thousands of tokens" of coherent thinking over hours, and a dedicated 1M-context production endpoint. Poolside states plainly that "extending to a 1M-token context window enables the strongest performance on hard tasks". Capped because there is **no MRCR, RULER, needle-retrieval or AA-LCR figure of any kind** — long-context *generation* is demonstrated, long-context *recall* is not measured at all — the free endpoint is limited to 256K, and no max-output figure is published.
- **Multimodal: 15/100.** None, and Poolside says so directly ("its lack of vision capabilities"). Text in, text out. Template floor. Worth recording the workaround as a capability note rather than a score: when it needed to check rendered output, it **drove headless Chromium to read canvases back and diffed screenshots numerically** — competent engineering around a hard limitation, but not multimodality.
- **Coding: 88/100.** The model's entire purpose, and the numbers justify the claim. **SWE-bench Multilingual 78.5% is the highest figure on Poolside's own comparison table**, ahead of Qwen 3.7 Max (78.3) and a 1.6T DeepSeek (76.2). **SWE-bench Pro 59.4%** is strong on a benchmark where most models struggle. **DeepSWE v1.1 40.4%** beats Gemini 3.5 Flash (37.0), Kimi K2.7 Code (31.0), Gemini 3.1 Pro (12.0) and DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Max (9.0) — on the one benchmark in the set that still spreads models out. The post-training corpus behind this is specific and credible: **38,000 real commits across 17,000 repositories, 168k software-engineering environments, 83k terminal environments, and a new agentic repository-installation task**. The harness-optimization case study — 5.2% faster, 71% fewer allocations, race-detector validated — is real engineering work, not a demo. Capped below 90 only because **nothing here is independently verified** and SWE-bench Pro remains 20 points behind the frontier.
- **Cost efficiency: 96/100.** As close to ideal as this dataset gets. **OpenMDW-1.1 open weights** published in **BF16, FP8, INT4 and NVFP4 with official GGUF and MLX conversions and official DFlash draft models**; **8B active parameters, so it runs on a single NVIDIA DGX Spark**; **$0.10 in / $0.20 out / $0.01 cache read** on the hosted 1M endpoint and a **free 256K endpoint**; day-one vLLM, SGLang, Ollama and TRT-LLM support; NeMo AutoModel and Prime Lab post-training paths; and **the pre-post-training base weights available on request**, which almost nobody offers. Delivering 70.2% Terminal-Bench and 78.5% SWE-bench Multilingual at this size and price is the best capability-per-dollar result I have assessed. Docked marginally because thinking mode — which is where the quality lives — costs 2–2.5× the tokens, so the effective price is higher than the headline.
- **Overall Score: 65.4/100.** Mean of the five non-cost dims (80 + 68 + 76 + 15 + 88) / 5 = 65.4. The multimodal floor and the complete absence of general-reasoning benchmarks hold the mean down; on coding and agentic work specifically this is an outstanding model. Best fit: **local and self-hosted agentic software engineering** — repo-scale changes, terminal-driven long-horizon tasks, dependency installation and test-suite repair, codebase Q&A — on a single workstation-class accelerator, with a free hosted endpoint for evaluation. Practical advice: **leave thinking on** (it is worth +23.9 points on DeepSWE), **prefer Poolside's own harness or expect first-call tool-schema errors** in third-party scaffolds, and **avoid it for anything requiring vision or broad factual knowledge**. Finally, a note on trust: Poolside publishes every evaluation trajectory, discloses a >50% reward-hacking spike it then fixed, and flags that its own DeepSWE harness differs from the official one. That standard of disclosure is rare, and it is the main reason I am comfortable scoring a set of entirely vendor-reported numbers this highly.
